As a pathologist, I studied disease through a microscope. As an epidemiologist, I looked for patterns across communities.

Then it became personal.

I lost my job, and my family lost our health insurance. The next month, my husband was bitten by a tick and developed Lyme disease.

That experience taught me how difficult it can be to find care while worrying about costs—and how language can make those conversations harder.

I kept thinking: how could we help another family feel better prepared?

Those lessons led me to build PathwayAI.

A medical bill shows only part of what illness costs. What about missed work, travel, and unpaid help from family?

PathwayAI helps people make that burden visible—and connects practical support with evidence for community planning.

Let me show you.

Choose what would help today: outdoor preparation, help after a tick bite, preparing for care, or community planning. Quick and easy gives shorter explanations.

Learn and Prevent offers a downloadable outdoor plan. Tick Bite: What Next explains removal, what to record, and when to contact a clinician. Early, appropriate treatment can help prevent more severe Lyme disease.

In Care and Support, people without insurance can find community health centers and questions about affordable care. Early Lyme disease can often be managed in primary care. Ask the center about evaluation, treatment, referrals, fees, and interpreters. Services vary; care is not automatically free.

Now imagine preparing for an appointment. Every patient detail in this demonstration is fictional.

Enter what happened. Review and correct the timeline. Download the visit summary and bring the original test report.

For someone uncomfortable speaking English, explaining symptoms can feel overwhelming. People can write in their preferred language or mix languages.

With permission, optional external AI helps prepare an English summary alongside the original for review. It supports preparation, not diagnosis or qualified medical interpretation. The English organizer also works without external AI.

Now look at what the bill misses.

Our fictional patient paid 180 dollars for care and 45 dollars for travel, missed three workdays, and received eight hours of family help.

That is 225 dollars paid. Workdays and caregiving hours stay separate.

Download the appointment and cost summary. Use the care-access checklist when calling a clinic or billing office.

A total alone will not pay a bill. A clearer record can help someone explain what support they need.

What can a community do?

County health departments and community partners are our intended organizational users. Public data helps them frame decisions about limited resources.

For example, CDC PLACES estimates that 6.5 percent of Dutchess adults aged 18 to 64 lacked health insurance in 2023. The county health plan also identifies access and coverage concerns. These are community findings, not Lyme-specific estimates.

They give us a reason to explore care navigation—and a question to test.

PathwayAI combines New York Lyme surveillance with Census population and language context, HRSA healthcare resources, CDC tick surveillance, and CDC PLACES estimates. CMS adds separate Original Medicare payment context—not Lyme-specific spending.

One calculation is fully visible: 1,060 Dutchess cases reported in 2024, multiplied by Hook’s societal-cost benchmark of 2,032 dollars, gives about 2.15 million dollars, expressed in 2016 dollars.

That is a study-based illustration, not measured county spending. Our survey highlights experiences to investigate. Horn’s research supports attention to follow-up needs. Neither is added to that dollar total.

Then we connect evidence with a possible action.

At local events, partners could introduce prevention information and preparation tools. Residents could leave with a plan, a visit note, or questions about affordable care.

A future secure pilot, with explicit consent, could document unmet needs. Resources would remain available without contributing personal information. Personal notes are not automatically sent to counties.

Partners would confirm service capacity and resources, then track referrals completed, appointments reached, and needs still unmet.

People living with Lyme disease and a county health commissioner reviewed the working app. Their feedback helped us simplify instructions and make resources available without entering a story.

Mandarin-, Spanish-, and English-speaking reviewers have also been invited. That testing is ongoing.

The app is publicly accessible. We welcome patient groups, health centers, and community partners for a small pilot. We will test usefulness before claiming better outcomes or savings.

Grants and partnerships are our proposed model for keeping patient access free. The structure could be adapted to other illnesses after condition-specific review.

AI can help organize words. Data can reveal patterns. Patients remain at the center.
