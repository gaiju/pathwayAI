Proposed Solution
When we started developing ImpactAtlas, we focused on one basic question: What does being sick actually cost someone?

The answer is usually much bigger than the medical bill.

A person can spend months, sometimes years, trying to understand what is wrong. During that time, there are doctor visits, diagnostic tests, treatments, transportation expenses, insurance coverage limitations, missed work, lost income, and countless hours spent navigating healthcare. Family members may become caregivers, education can be interrupted, and everyday activities may become difficult.

These challenges are particularly significant for people experiencing invisible illnesses, chronic conditions, delayed diagnoses, or unresolved symptoms, including Lyme disease, Long COVID, ME/CFS, temporomandibular disorders (TMD/TMJD), chronic spinal disorders, postural abnormalities, and craniofacial pain.

Many of these costs are not fully represented in traditional healthcare expenditure data.

We developed ImpactAtlas to bring these missing pieces together.

Patients can describe their experiences through voice or text without needing to complete complicated medical or economic forms. The platform organizes their information across our 15 dimensions of Cost of Illness intelligence:

Patient Profile and Context

Patient Voice and Lived Experience

Symptoms and Functional Burden

Diagnostic Journey

Healthcare and Treatment Journey

Direct Medical Costs

Insurance and Patient Financial Responsibility

Patient Time and Administrative Burden

Work and Productivity Burden

Family and Caregiver Burden

Transportation and Access Burden

Total Cost of Illness

Recovery and Future Cost Scenarios

Federal Data and AI Intelligence

Personalized Action Plan and Follow Up

ImpactAtlas builds a structured timeline of the patient’s diagnostic and healthcare journey, identifies important missing information, and examines the complete medical, financial, functional, and societal burden of illness.

The platform connects patient information with relevant U.S. federal open data and health economic evidence, including AHRQ MEPS, CDC, CMS, the U.S. Census Bureau, Bureau of Labor Statistics, HRSA, HealthData.gov, Data.gov, and peer reviewed research.

However, federal datasets have limitations. Some provide population level disease prevalence, healthcare expenditures, employment statistics, insurance information, and geographic comparisons but do not capture individual diagnostic delays, caregiver burden, uncovered treatments, personal financial losses, or the full effects of chronic illness on daily life.

To address these gaps, ImpactAtlas uses two complementary approaches:

Evidence Based Analysis: When relevant information is available, the platform uses patient reported information, documented expenses, published research, and applicable datasets to generate supported calculations and insights.

AI Assisted Simulation: When information is incomplete or unavailable, the platform can generate modeled scenarios using available evidence and explicit assumptions. These estimates are clearly distinguished from observed facts, allowing patients to understand both the potential burden and the uncertainty involved.

Results are identified as OBSERVED, DERIVED, MODELED, or UNAVAILABLE, providing transparency about the evidence supporting each calculation.

ImpactAtlas also addresses insurance coverage gaps and financial barriers associated with chronic conditions. Treatments such as TMJD appliances, chiropractic services, specialized rehabilitation, physical therapy, and certain chronic pain treatments may have coverage restrictions, visit limits, or exclusions depending on the patient’s insurance plan.

The platform helps patients organize these expenses, identify potential coverage gaps, understand their financial burden, and prepare questions for healthcare professionals or insurers.

For patients experiencing unexplained symptoms or delayed diagnoses, ImpactAtlas organizes their symptom history, identifies information gaps, and provides personalized recommendations for further discussion with qualified healthcare professionals. It supports informed decision making without replacing medical diagnosis.

At the end of the process, patients receive a comprehensive report containing their diagnostic journey, medical and treatment history, multidimensional Cost of Illness analysis, direct and indirect financial burden, evidence sources, modeled scenarios where applicable, and a personalized action plan with recommended follow up considerations.

Beyond individual patients, ImpactAtlas provides a reusable framework for researchers, healthcare organizations, patient advocates, and government agencies. The platform can support comparisons across diseases, geographic locations, and populations, helping identify patterns in disease burden, healthcare access, economic impact, and unmet patient needs.

ImpactAtlas is already operating as a web application. Since its launch on October 17, 2026, the platform has attracted early users and has been tested with patients and healthcare professionals, including a doctor, nurse, and certified nursing assistant. Their participation supports ongoing improvements in usability, clarity, patient workflows, and reporting.

Our long term objective is to expand the platform across chronic and invisible illnesses, strengthen its evidence and economic models, support healthcare and research partnerships, and develop population level intelligence that can inform public health decisions.

ImpactAtlas makes the invisible medical, financial, functional, and societal burden of illness visible, measurable, and actionable.

Pricing the Invisible, Valuing the Cure

Quantifying the Human and Economic Costs of Invisible Illness

A Health Policy Decision Console by Healthful Data

Overview
Invisible illnesses are costly and poorly counted, so they are hard to fund or prioritize. We built a working policy console that answers one question for Long COVID: what would a cure be worth to the United States?

A policymaker or analyst sets a few levers: treatment effectiveness, share of the affected population reached, time horizon (1, 10, or 20 years), and discounted or undiscounted value. The console returns a national value with a range. In our base case, a hypothetical treatment that is 75% effective and reaches 65% of people living with Long COVID is worth $5.32 trillion over 20 years at a 3% discount rate. The range is $3.09T to $7.55T if any single parameter is taken to its evidence extreme. The widest driver is willingness to pay per QALY.

The model is built to be audited and disputed. Every number traces to a source, and an analyst can switch sources on or off, add their own, and see how the result changes.

Alongside the national burden and decision console, we built a community-led data-collection layer: the Community-Led Long COVID Burden Index (CLCBI) built off our team's expertise in developing and deploying similar models globally for the HIV pandemic and vetted by a half dozen individuals living with ME/CFS and Long COVID via the Science 4 Me (S4Me.info) forum and A Million Points of Light, a campaign and map that lets people with invisible illness be counted that includes several real lived experiences from individuals living with invisible illness from our team's sister nonprofit, Healthful Stories (HealthfulHeroes.com).

1. Results for the American People
The problem. Federal data systems capture invisible illness poorly. Claims and diagnosis codes miss most people with Long COVID, and the most severely affected are the least likely to appear in any survey. Without a defensible cost-of-illness figure, the burden is easy to overlook in funding and policy decisions.

What we delivered.

A national valuation of the human and economic burden of Long COVID. It covers value of health recovered (QALYs), medical spending avoided, national lost earnings, and wages recovered.

Three distinct definitions of the affected population, so policymakers can see how much the answer depends on who is counted:

People who self-report Long COVID, whether or not they were symptomatic at the time of infection.

People with symptomatic COVID-19 who self-report Long COVID.

Diagnosed Long COVID, identified by U09 ICD-10 codes. We expect this scope to substantially undercount.

Quality-of-life loss per person, measured against age-matched population utility.

Burden modeled over time, including the annual rate of change in prevalence for each population definition.

Lived experience. The CLCBI is a community-led instrument. It invites caretakers to respond on behalf of people in their care, and we state openly that the most severely affected remain under-represented, so burden is likely under-quantified. A Million Points of Light adds a public, persistent map where people see they are not alone, with each of 17 invisible illnesses glowing a different color. Both are designed so lived experience feeds the model directly.

Of note, the Community-Led COVID Burden Index is designed for anyone who has ever been diagnosed with a COVID infection. For those who are unaware of Long COVID, it provides a knowledge assessment and offers recommendations on how they might increase their knowledge. It also offers a risk score based on modifiable and unmodifiable features of risk to ensure that individuals with higher risk of contracting Long COVID are aware of those risks. Uniquely, those who we establish as having features of Long COVID can be activated toward care.

Note that the CLCBI was also vetted with input from the Science 4 ME (S4Me.info forum) and after conversations with leading experts such as Professor Lenny Jason and Professor Matt Mazewski among others.

2. Data, Tech & Evidence-Driven Innovation
U.S. open data. We wired in MEPS and Household Pulse Survey data directly and reviewed each by hand to build the population scopes and prevalence trends. Our evidence pipeline screened roughly 6,500 data sources. We ingested the clinical trial landscape (ClinicalTrials.gov) to show where treatment development stands, and we connect the fragmented sources into one valuation.

AI-accelerated evidence runner. An AI-assisted literature-discovery and extraction pipeline (PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov) pulls candidate estimates for each parameter. It combines multiple sources using inverse-variance pooling and records the disagreement range between them.

Source register and transparency. A searchable register shows every candidate source, its extracted value, the supporting quote, a link, and its review status. Analysts can include or exclude sources, add their own, and watch the headline update.

Sensitivity analysis. A tornado chart shows how much each parameter moves the result. This makes the evidence gaps visible. Several key multipliers still rest on a single source, and the console flags them as validation priorities.

Discounting. Discount rates are set by scenario and sourced through the same evidence runner. The headline range shows how far the answer moves under different assumptions.

3. Execution, Efficiency & Delivery
Working today.

The live decision console runs end to end. It includes the calculation view, source register, breakdown, and a “How it works” explainer.

The CLCBI is live on the Healthful platform, and the Million Points of Light signup page and map are live.

Thrive.JoinHealthful.com has already served over 12,000 individuals in the US and with proper financing, we are excited to expand it to create a longitudinal cohort of 10,000 individuals living with Long COVID over the two years.

Near-term use. An analyst can use the console today to:

test how a cure or treatment scenario changes national value;

find which assumptions drive the answer;

justify where new data collection would reduce uncertainty most.

What we scoped deliberately. The MVP covers the core valuation and the time view. Additional modules (cure R&D return, cure timeline, downstream excess disease risk, program costs) are specified and sequenced on top of the same evidence-runner architecture.

4. Scalability, Reuse & Societal Impact
Reusable by design. Nothing in the architecture is specific to Long COVID. The condition selector, population scopes, utility estimates, cost parameters, and evidence runner are generic components. Adding ME/CFS or another chronic condition means supplying new population, utility, and cost inputs, not rebuilding the model. The selector already lists additional conditions as demonstration entries “in development.”

Policy application. The same logic supports:

research prioritization across conditions, comparing cure value per dollar of R&D;

coverage and program decisions, by showing medical spending avoided;

regulatory and benefit-cost analysis, using a transparent QALY valuation including those supported by US HHS.

Standards alignment. The CLCBI and the evidence register use structured, auditable records that can be mapped to federal data standards. Downloads are structured into FHIR format

Community scale. A Million Points of Light is an open invitation for people with invisible illness to be counted, and it works across all 17 listed conditions. It gives the model a growing, community-sourced base for future parameters. The Community Led COVID Burden Index runs on the same platform that has already proven valuable to over 12,000 users and delivers value regardless of Long COVID, ME/CFS, or other invisible illness status. That is, for the many millions who remain undiagnosed, a risk score helps to encourage them to take precautions or seek clinical evaluation for symptoms which persist 12 or more weeks after COVID infection. We can adjust these triggers over time as the science evolves.

Every figure on this page can be traced back to a document or a dataset, and every stage below is built so the trace cannot break quietly. That is the architecture in one sentence: it is designed to make an untraceable number impossible rather than unlikely.

Results for the American People
Target users. Federal health economists and budget analysts (ASPE, CMS, NIH, CBO), congressional and agency staff building the case for research funding, state health departments, and patient advocacy organizations who need a defensible number rather than an anecdote.

The workflow it fits. An analyst is asked what a Long COVID treatment would be worth, and has days, not months.

Today that means assembling scattered published estimates, inheriting each author’s population definition, and producing a figure nobody downstream can interrogate.

With the console, the analyst opens one page and:

Picks the population definition — self-reported, symptomatic-and-self-reported, or U09-diagnosed — and sees how much the answer depends on who is counted.

Sets effectiveness, reach, horizon, and discount rate to match the scenario being costed.

Clicks any number to see the source behind it, the verbatim quote, and the analyst who verified it.

Disagrees. Switches a source off, adds their own, and regenerates the total rather than rebuilding the model.

Reads the tornado chart to see which evidence gap moves the result, which tells them where new data collection is worth funding.

Exports a FHIR R4B bundle so the figure arrives in their own system with its derivation intact.

For patients and advocates, the Community-Led Long COVID Burden Index and A Million Points of Light turn lived experience into counted evidence, so the people the number describes help produce it.


Data, Tech & Evidence Driven Approach
Our model uses open federal data, calculated from primary sources rather than secondary reports. Seventeen connectors pull raw data from NHIS and MEPS microdata, the Census Household Pulse Survey, BLS earnings series, Census population projections, NIH RePORTER, openFDA, ClinicalTrials.gov, and other sources; each computes its own values. We calculate variance using each survey’s design, including published strata and primary sampling units or replicate weights. Each pull logs a SHA-256 hash. Automated tests continually re-pull and recalculate data, breaking the build if a source number changes.

AI is used only for peer-reviewed literature. It searches databases, screens abstracts against parameter definitions, and proposes values with verbatim quotes and page locators. In the latest run, it screened 6,019 papers across 6,492 decisions, logged 6,339 distinct exclusion reasons, and found four usable records. Rejections are visible in the tool. AI cannot decide: each proposal stays in a review queue until a named analyst verifies and promotes it, logging their name, timestamp, and reason. A separate check re-fetches documents to confirm quotes verbatim (25 of 25 in use; none unreachable). Nineteen more checks test whether valid numbers are believable, catching double-counted samples and dollar figures without a price year.

We also include one proprietary dataset: a COVID health-impact assessment hosted on Healthful. We query it read-only through an allow-listed crosswalk and aggregate outputs above strict minimum cell thresholds.

The pipeline exports as a 140-entry FHIR R4B Bundle, validated in tests and at runtime. Each Observation links to its source via derivedFrom, letting anyone trace final math to the raw survey file or exact sentence in a paper.


Execution, Efficiency & Delivery

The most useful thing the tool shows is where the money is not. Of a $635.2 billion annual burden for long COVID, medical spending is 7.2%. Lost earnings are 31.3% and lost quality of life is 61.5%, so any analysis counting only healthcare costs misses nine-tenths of the problem and goes looking for savings in the smallest layer. The second is proportion. Mean NIH funding for the condition was $251 million a year across FY2020 to FY2026, read from RePORTER. The annual burden is about 2,500 times that. For a decision maker the answer is not one number but a dependency. The horizon and the discount rate are controls, so the same evidence can be read over one, ten or twenty years, discounted at OMB's 3% or not at all, and the published range of $3.09 trillion to $7.55 trillion names willingness to pay per QALY as the input that moves it most. That tells a budget holder what to argue about. Because every figure clicks through to the sentence it came from, disagreement gets specific instead of general. An independently published model estimates 865,000 QALYs lost a year; ours implies 891,593, which we found afterwards rather than by tuning. The export is a FHIR R4B Bundle, so another system can ingest the result and walk the arithmetic behind it. Where an input is missing the tool shows the layers it can compute and names what is absent, which is what makes the numbers it does show worth using.


Scalability, Reuse & Societal Impact:
Chronic illness carries a hidden price tag. By counting only hospital bills and prescriptions, policymakers miss over 90% of the cost to American families: lost wages, daily function, and quality of life. We built this platform to make that burden visible and verifiable.

Rather than create a one-disease calculator, we built shared public infrastructure for chronic illness research. Our live MongoDB backend and automated validation engine run over 1,300 continuous checks. Because the engine is disease-agnostic, new conditions require configuration, not custom engineering. We expanded from Long COVID to Lyme disease and ME/CFS without changing core code. Open YAML and standard FHIR formats let patient organizations and research teams use our work.

Honest science means naming blind spots. Formal diagnoses make our numbers reflect healthcare access, not everyone who is sick. Black Americans, for example, face Lyme exposure risks comparable to White Americans but are diagnosed far less often. We also rely too heavily on cross-sectional snapshots and limited quality-of-life studies. Next, we’ll add validated quality-of-life instruments to patient surveys, state-level CDC data for local health departments, and evaluations of real-world clinical programs that help patients today—not just the abstract value of a total cure.

ImpactFile — Build Evidence. Submit Proof.

ImpactFile is a working iPhone application that transforms everyday experiences of illness into organized, shareable evidence packets for medical leave, disability benefits, insurance appeals, and tax documentation.

The Output: ImpactFile currently supports four purpose-built documentation packets:

FMLA: Medical leave documentation covering symptoms, treatment, functional limitations, and missed work.

Visible Burden AI: Making the Full Burden of Invisible Illness Visible Over Time
by
Latina Wright-Booker
Latina Wright-Booker
|
2 weeks ago
|
in Cost of Illness: TOPx Submission
Approved
Wright-Booker Innovation Lab (WBIL) is an intergenerational innovation team committed to developing practical, human-centered solutions that improve lives through data, technology, and systems thinking. Our guiding principle is Innovation with Purpose. Solutions with Impact.

Our team brings experience in public service, psychology, strategic planning, systems navigation, community engagement, research synthesis, and technology-assisted problem solving.

Our Phase 2 solution, Visible Burden AI (VBAI), addresses a critical gap in how invisible illness is understood.

People living with Long COVID and other invisible illnesses experience consequences that extend far beyond clinical symptoms or medical expenses. Employment disruptions, financial strain, caregiving responsibilities, healthcare navigation, lost time, and emotional impacts are frequently documented separately or not documented at all.

The burden isn't simply invisible. It's fragmented.

VBAI is a patient-centered, interactive web application designed to make these disconnected experiences understandable through a multidimensional Burden Portrait.

The prototype captures self-reported experiences across seven dimensions: health, financial, employment, caregiving, navigation, time, and emotional/social impact. It also demonstrates how burden can change over time and incorporates U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey data to provide relevant population level context.

Importantly, VBAI maintains a clear separation between an individual's reported experience and federal population statistics. Community data provides context; it does not determine or validate an individual's burden.

Our initial focus is Long COVID, with a design intended to accommodate additional invisible illnesses.

A medical encounter captures a moment. Visible Burden AI helps reveal what happens between the moments.

The ultimate goal is to help individuals better understand and communicate the cumulative impact of illness while supporting future opportunities for informed conversations with caregivers, clinicians, employers, and benefits navigators.

Solution Overview (MVP Demonstration):
User-Facing Application

Live demo, prototype, or working product
https://visible-burden-ai.streamlit.app/

Access instructions (if needed)
Visible Burden AI is a browser-based prototype. Open the application using the provided link; no installation is required.

Begin with the introductory screen, then navigate to the Daily Check-In to enter sample experiences across the burden dimensions. Generate the Burden Portrait to view the multidimensional summary.

Explore the longitudinal demonstration to see how burden may change over time, and review Community Context to see county-level U.S. Census American Community Survey measures displayed separately from individual responses.

The longitudinal examples use synthetic demonstration data. VBAI is a decision support prototype, not a diagnostic or clinically validated assessment tool.

Note: We will revise these instructions if the deployed application's navigation differs from this description.

Results for the American People
Millions of Americans living with invisible illnesses experience burdens that extend beyond medical symptoms and expenses. Yet these impacts are often fragmented across healthcare, employment, caregiving, and household systems.

Visible Burden AI (VBAI) is designed primarily for adults living with invisible illnesses, beginning with Long COVID. Potential secondary users include caregivers, clinicians, employers, and benefits navigators.

In a typical workflow, an individual completes a brief Daily Check-In describing how illness affects different areas of life. VBAI translates these self-reported experiences into a seven-dimensional Burden Portrait covering health, financial, employment, caregiving, navigation, time, and emotional/social impacts.

A longitudinal view demonstrates how burden can fluctuate over time. Separate Census-based Community Context helps users understand relevant population conditions without treating those averages as personal circumstances.

VBAI offers a more complete picture of what illness is costing a person's life not simply what medical care costs.

The intended benefit is greater awareness, clearer communication, and more informed conversations about support needs.

A medical encounter captures a moment. VBAI helps reveal what happens between the moments.

Data, Tech & Evidence Driven Approach
Visible Burden AI combines structured self-reported information, transparent prototype logic, longitudinal visualization, and federal open data context.

The working MVP is built in Python using Streamlit. Users enter experiences through a Daily Check-In, which organizes responses into seven burden dimensions and generates an interactive Burden Portrait.

The current prototype uses rules-based processing rather than a clinically validated scoring instrument or deployed generative AI model. Synthetic data demonstrates how an individual's burden may fluctuate across time.

VBAI integrates publicly available U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey (ACS) data to provide county level economic and healthcare access context, including household income, employment, and health insurance indicators.

Federal population statistics remain explicitly separate from self-reported individual burden. They provide context but do not calculate, validate, or determine a person's experience.

Development has been informed by five qualitative stakeholder perspectives: patient, caregiver, employer, clinician, and benefits navigator along with federal expert and patient-advocate feedback during the Sprint.

This feedback shaped the seven-dimensional Burden Portrait, longitudinal demonstration, and contextual data presentation.

The architecture supports future development of responsible AI-assisted interpretation, additional federal datasets, accessibility enhancements, and interoperability pathways. These capabilities are prospective and are not represented as completed integrations.

Execution, Efficiency & Delivery
Visible Burden AI demonstrates a practical approach to making the fragmented consequences of invisible illness easier to recognize and communicate.

The MVP converts structured daily experiences into a multidimensional Burden Portrait, reducing the need to interpret disconnected information across separate areas of life.

Its longitudinal demonstration illustrates how burden can change between medical encounters, revealing patterns that a single appointment or expense estimate may overlook.

County-level Census context adds relevant population information while preserving the distinction between public statistics and individual experience.

The project progressed through concept development, qualitative stakeholder research, Alpha prototyping, and a working Beta demonstration. Federal expert and patient-advocate feedback informed refinements to usability, data interpretation, and longitudinal presentation.

The Streamlit-based implementation provides a lightweight foundation for testing and iteration without requiring complex infrastructure.

VBAI's current value is improved visibility and understanding not a claimed reduction in medical spending or a validated clinical outcome.

Its longer term potential includes supporting clearer conversations about work limitations, caregiving responsibilities, navigation challenges, and changing support needs.

The goal is to move from information to understanding and ultimately to informed action.

Scalability, Reuse & Societal Impact:
Visible Burden AI is designed as a modular, patient-centered platform that can expand beyond Long COVID to other invisible illnesses, including ME/CFS and Lyme disease.

Its seven dimensional burden framework provides a reusable structure for organizing self reported experiences while supporting future condition specific adaptations.

The Python/Streamlit architecture supports iterative development and deployment. Federal population data can be incorporated through documented integrations while preserving the distinction between individual burden and population context.

Future priorities include accessibility improvements, validation with patients and researchers, stronger longitudinal interpretation, additional federal datasets, and appropriate interoperability pathways.

Further user research will help ensure the platform remains accessible to people experiencing fatigue, cognitive limitations, or sensory sensitivities.

Potential applications include patient self-advocacy, caregiver communication, healthcare discussions, workplace accommodations, and benefits navigation. These uses require further testing and do not represent existing automated clinical or eligibility systems.

VBAI's societal value lies in making overlooked consequences of invisible illness understandable, helping individuals and support systems recognize needs and identify opportunities for meaningful action.

DBbun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator
by
Uri Kartoun
Uri Kartoun
|
2 weeks ago
|
in Cost of Illness: TOPx Submission
Approved
Solo entry by Uri Kartoun, PhD, founder of DBbun LLC, Cambridge, Massachusetts. DBbun won Phase 1 and is participating in Phase 2.

Proposed solution: the DBbun Invisible Illness Burden Simulator turns a published paper on Lyme disease or Long COVID into a live what-if simulator whose every value is labeled observed, assumed or projected, with federal context beside it. The public demo has 25 finished simulators and 13 cross-pollination analyses, each combining a Lyme disease paper and a Long COVID paper.

Solution Overview (MVP Demonstration):
User-Facing Application

Live demo, prototype, or working product
https://dbbun.github.io/dbbun-invisible-illness-burden-simulator/

Access instructions (if needed)
On the demo's Simulation Library, open simulator 3 (MyLymeData). Read Overview, switch to the Care delivery teams view, then open Explore, change one assumption, and see the Export tab. Press play on its audio overview and open the transcript. Open simulator 2 and read Evidence & Sources for the cost inputs and federal check. To see a cross-pollination analysis, go back to the Simulation Library and, under simulator 3, click "Cross-pollination analysis available: with 13" to open the analysis of simulators 3 and 13.

The public site is read-only; uploading a paper and the API run in the full tool on the author's computer.

Results for the American People
DBbun lets a patient advocate see which burdens a study's results do not cover, a care team see which assumption it must not treat as patient fact, and a policy analyst tell one study's figure from a population estimate before budgeting.

It turns a published paper into a simulator that labels each value observed, assumed or projected, shows federal context beside it, and lets anyone change an assumption.

I designed it by listening to people. I held eight interviews (six patient advocates, a Long COVID patient, a government deputy director) and posted 17 questions on the LymeX Healthathon forum; 15 received replies. After listening, I added a medical-disbelief category, a claims-undercounting check and an Excel export for budget analysts. For patients who may struggle with screens, pages fit phones and tablets, and every simulator has a spoken overview and transcript.

Across this non-systematically chosen library of 25 simulators, results are tagged to 1.4 of 16 burden categories on average, none to more than 3. Six categories have no tagged result for either condition, among them three that people described to me: care continuity, parent-to-child transmission and long-term opportunity costs.

Thirteen cross-pollination analyses pair a Lyme and a Long COVID paper and ask what one condition could learn from the other. Six Lyme simulators report a diagnostic-delay result and no Long COVID simulator does: could Long COVID's time to diagnosis be measured?

Data, Tech & Evidence Driven Approach
Four Claude Sonnet calls extract findings with source locators, build an interactive simulator, write named scenarios and forecasts, and generate Python source. Each value is labeled observed, assumed or projected, and ordinary code does the arithmetic.

A local machine-learning text classifier, using term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) weighting, decides with no model call whether a document concerns Lyme disease or Long COVID. A retrieval scorer then selects up to ten units from a library of about 1,454, drawn from 30 federal dataset families across 12 agencies. A unit is a processed summary of one federal file, or of one part of a file. Federal data supplies context and checks; the paper supplies the inputs.

Code performs three federal operations: restating dollars with the BLS price index, comparing Lyme case counts with CDC surveillance, and comparing Long COVID prevalence with Household Pulse. Where a cited figure has no year, code declines to compare it rather than guess.

Example: Simulator 2's source reports $2,227 per Lyme episode in 2022 dollars; code restates it as about $2,450 in 2025 dollars, labeled general inflation, not medical-care inflation.

On the five simulators with forecasts, a Monte Carlo simulation of 500 random draws within each assumption's range shades the chart, labeled illustrative, not a confidence interval. Cross-pollination tables are computed in code; the model tags burdens and writes wording, which code checks against fixed lists.

Two guardrails: sex- and gender-specific findings are reported separately, and Long COVID figures sourced from claims or ICD-10 codes are flagged, because such codes capture under half of cases in the population studied by Tian et al. (JAMA Network Open, 2026).

Execution, Efficiency & Delivery
DBbun works as a public, read-only demonstration at https://dbbun.github.io/dbbun-invisible-illness-burden-simulator/. Anyone can open 25 simulators, change assumptions, read 13 saved cross-pollination analyses, hear spoken overviews, and download bundles. The use is evidence exploration: inspect a study's sources and limits, change an assumption, export the results.

Generating a new simulator from a paper, or up to five related documents, runs in the full tool on my computer, not on the public site. It takes about four to nine minutes and $0.40 to $0.96 in model fees per paper (average $0.69, 25 runs), excluding my review time, hosting and maintenance. A documented API, exercised locally, supports another system submitting papers and retrieving results. There is no public self-service upload yet.

After federal reviewers' feedback, I built a ranked list of federal sources, observed, assumed and projected labels, price-year and payer labels, and forecast bands. Five supporting documents are attached: feedback log, quality assurance, interoperability, policy brief, references.

AI critiques reviewed earlier versions of every simulator and cross-pollination analysis, and a separate AI code review (GPT-5 Codex) found ten issues: nine were fixed with regression tests, one is documented. All 25 simulators were then regenerated under the revised rules. 233 automated tests pass.

Scalability, Reuse & Societal Impact:
DBbun is infrastructure, not a one-disease tool: its extraction, evidence labels, arithmetic, API and FHIR export are not written for one disease; the curated federal evidence, prompts and burden categories are built for Lyme disease and Long COVID. To test reuse, I ran two collapsed-lung papers through it, changing only a condition label and scope statement. Each produced a labeled simulator, unvalidated. That shows initial reuse; a new condition needs curated evidence. Societal impact is the evidence gap: six of 16 burden categories have no tagged result for either condition, and the 2022 to 2025 National Health Interview Survey adult codebooks I reviewed have no Lyme question. Deployment: the demo is public and read-only; the full tool runs on one computer with a documented API. Next steps: a hosted version with self-service upload, CMS production access for Blue Button, and asking federal surveys to add items on caregiver hours and schooling disrupted. Standards: each model exports as HL7 FHIR R4 Observation resources with provenance to the source paper; all 26 files pass the official HL7 validator with no errors (warnings only; terminology checks off). Blue Button 2.0 was demonstrated against the CMS sandbox with synthetic beneficiaries, and the CMS Interoperability Framework's consent and transparency principles are echoed. The aim: a reusable evidence layer for any condition and its comorbidities, ideally across PubMed.

Health Worth

in Cost of Illness: TOPx Submission
Approved
Every day, Americans make healthcare decisions without understanding what those decisions may cost them. A diagnosis can introduce an overwhelming set of questions: What care will I need? What will it cost? What will insurance cover? Can I afford treatment? What happens if I wait?

Health Worth was created to help answer those questions.

Health Worth is a patient-centered healthcare decision intelligence platform that helps individuals understand the potential financial implications of a diagnosis, condition, or medical procedure, including the potential costs associated with delaying care.

Unlike traditional price transparency tools that focus primarily on individual services, Health Worth takes a broader approach by connecting healthcare pricing information, condition-specific care journeys, insurance information, and decision-support intelligence.

The platform enables users to explore potential care pathways, understand estimated costs, compare care settings, and evaluate how different decisions may influence their financial exposure.

Core capabilities include:

Condition-based care journeys: Explore potential services, procedures, and treatment pathways associated with specific diagnoses.

Episode-level cost intelligence: Understand estimated costs across care settings and services rather than relying solely on isolated procedure prices.

Cost-of-delay intelligence: Explore potential financial implications of postponing treatment, with scenario-based estimates rather than predictions of individual outcomes.

Insurance connectivity: Support patient-initiated connections to available payer data, including Medicare, to provide more personalized financial context.

Transparent estimates: Present cost ranges and underlying assumptions to help patients understand uncertainty.

Patient-centered experience: Translate complex healthcare financial information into understandable, actionable insights.

Health Worth integrates publicly available healthcare datasets, commercial pricing information, and patient-authorized insurance data where available.

The platform has progressed beyond conceptual development into a functioning MVP. Its development has been informed by initial research and subsequent usability testing focused on functionality and patient support.

Team and Leadership

Health Worth was founded and developed by a healthcare strategy and digital innovation leader with nearly 20 years of healthcare industry experience, an MPH, MBA, and graduate education in management and leadership, and ongoing doctoral research in public health.

The founder's professional experience spans healthcare technology, digital product strategy, consumer engagement, health information accessibility, payment integrity, and responsible AI implementation.

Our Vision

Healthcare decisions should not require patients to choose between financial uncertainty and their health.

Health Worth aims to make the financial implications of care more visible, understandable, and actionable, empowering Americans to make more informed healthcare decisions.

Solution Overview (MVP Demonstration):
User-Facing Application

Live demo, prototype, or working product
Health Worth is a functioning, web-based healthcare decision intelligence platform that enables users to explore condition-specific care journeys, understand estimated healthcare costs, and evaluate the potential financial implications of delaying treatment.

The MVP demonstrates condition exploration, episode-level pricing intelligence, scenario-based cost-of-delay analysis, and patient-initiated insurance connectivity where supported.

The demonstration follows a patient from identifying a condition through exploring potential care pathways, reviewing estimated costs, and understanding how treatment timing and insurance information may affect financial decisions.

Live demo / working product:

https://www.myhealthworth.com

Access instructions (if needed)
Access Health Worth using a modern desktop or mobile browser at https://www.myhealthworth.com.

Explore the available condition journeys and pricing features. Where supported, users may initiate an insurance connection to access additional personalized information.

The demonstration can be completed without connecting a personal insurance account.

Results for the American People
Health Worth addresses a fundamental challenge facing Americans: making healthcare decisions without understanding the financial consequences of receiving care or delaying it.

The primary users are patients managing a diagnosis, considering a procedure, or evaluating treatment options. Caregivers and family members can also use the platform to support healthcare decisions.

A patient begins by selecting a condition or procedure, such as knee osteoarthritis or knee replacement. Health Worth presents a potential care journey, estimates treatment costs, and helps the patient explore how different care settings, insurance circumstances, and treatment timelines may affect financial exposure.

Importantly, Health Worth goes beyond medical bills to consider the broader financial implications of care, including lost income, caregiving needs, and potential downstream costs.

The solution was informed by patient-centered research, including qualitative interviews and quantitative surveys. A subsequent usability study evaluated platform functionality and its ability to support patients.

Health Worth helps Americans move from uncertainty toward informed conversations with their healthcare providers and insurers.

Data, Tech & Evidence Driven Approach
Health Worth combines federal open data, healthcare pricing information, clinical evidence, and modern technology to translate fragmented healthcare information into actionable financial intelligence.

The platform incorporates CMS Medicare payment and claims-related information, hospital price transparency data, commercial healthcare pricing, and federal resources supporting medication, productivity, and healthcare utilization estimates.

Condition-specific care journeys organize relevant services and potential treatment pathways. Episode-level pricing models estimate costs across care settings, while scenario-based calculations illustrate how delaying treatment may influence future healthcare spending and indirect financial burdens.

Patient-initiated insurance connectivity uses standards-based authorization and FHIR-compatible data exchange where supported. The MVP includes Medicare and payer connectivity demonstrated through a sandbox environment using synthetic coverage and claims information.

Health Worth emphasizes transparency. Estimates incorporate ranges, assumptions, data provenance, and methodological limitations rather than presenting modeled outcomes as guaranteed costs.

Development was informed by initial patient research identifying the importance of trustworthy estimates, understandable assumptions, and insurance context. A separate usability study subsequently evaluated the functioning platform.

The result is a working application that transforms complex healthcare datasets into understandable, patient-centered decision support.

Execution, Efficiency & Delivery
Health Worth demonstrates how existing healthcare data can be transformed into a practical tool that supports more informed decisions without requiring patients to become experts in medical billing or insurance.

The functioning MVP includes condition-specific care journeys, episode-level cost estimates, comparisons across care settings, cost-of-delay scenarios, and patient-initiated insurance connectivity.

Rather than requiring patients to independently research medical prices, treatment pathways, insurance information, and potential financial consequences, Health Worth brings these elements into a unified experience.

The platform was developed through iterative product delivery, progressing from research and concept validation to a functional application and usability evaluation.

Its primary value is improved decision-making: helping patients understand potential financial exposure, recognize important cost drivers, and prepare more informed questions for providers and insurers.

Health Worth also demonstrates a reusable technical approach to organizing fragmented healthcare data into understandable financial insights.

While measurable downstream outcomes require additional validation, the MVP establishes a foundation for evaluating improvements in cost comprehension, decision confidence, and financial preparedness.

Scalability, Reuse & Societal Impact:
Health Worth was designed as an extensible platform rather than a single-condition cost calculator.

Its modular architecture supports the addition of diagnoses, procedures, care pathways, pricing datasets, and insurance integrations without rebuilding the core experience.

The current platform supports 27 condition journeys spanning cancer, cardiovascular and metabolic disease, neurological and mental health conditions, respiratory illness, musculoskeletal disorders, kidney disease, and infectious disease.

The near-term roadmap targets expansion to 100 supported diagnoses, broader payer connectivity, enhanced geographic pricing intelligence, and additional validation of cost and treatment-delay models. Future development will prioritize clinical and economic evidence review, patient usability, accessibility, privacy, and security.

Health Worth has potential applications across patient advocacy organizations, community health programs, healthcare navigation services, and consumer-facing digital health platforms.

Its broader societal opportunity is to make healthcare financial information more accessible, understandable, and useful, particularly for individuals facing complex or financially consequential healthcare decisions.

By helping people understand both the cost of receiving care and the potential cost of waiting, Health Worth aims to support greater financial preparedness and informed healthcare decisions.

Trisha, your page asks “What happens if I wait?”, and one published answer for Lyme disease is in the claims data: the average direct medical cost per episode was $695 for localized disease, $2,227 across all episodes, and $6,833 for disseminated disease, in 2022 dollars (chart attached). It’s an association, so it doesn’t prove that waiting causes the higher cost, and it counts direct medical costs only, not lost work. But it is the kind of figure that can anchor a cost-of-delay scenario, and it supports your choice to present scenario-based estimates and not predictions for any one person.

The figures come from simulator 2 in my entry (Yu et al., JAMA Netw Open 2026), where each value is labeled as observed, assumed or projected, and dollar amounts can be restated to current prices by code, for example $2,227 in 2022 dollars is about $2,450 in 2025 dollars, labeled as general inflation, not medical-care inflation.
