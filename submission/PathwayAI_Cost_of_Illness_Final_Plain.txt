As a pathologist, I studied disease through a microscope. As an epidemiologist, I looked for patterns across communities.

Then it became personal.

I lost my job, and my family lost our health insurance. The next month, my husband was bitten by a tick and developed Lyme disease.

That experience taught me how difficult it can be to find care while worrying about costs—and how language can make those conversations harder.

I kept thinking: how could we help another family feel better prepared?

I’m Yuwei Zhang. Those lessons led me to build PathwayAI.

A medical bill shows only part of what illness costs. What about the work you miss? The travel? The family member who gives up their time to help?

PathwayAI helps patients put those experiences into a useful record—and helps communities ask where support is needed.

Let’s start with a person who needs care.

In Care and Support, someone without insurance can find community health centers and questions about affordable care. Early Lyme disease can often be managed in primary care. We help people ask about evaluation, treatment, referrals, fees, and interpreters. Services vary; care is not automatically free.

We also help people prepare before illness. Learn and Prevent offers a downloadable outdoor plan. Tick Bite: What Next explains removal, what to record, and when to contact a clinician. Early, appropriate treatment can help prevent more severe Lyme disease.

Now imagine preparing for an appointment. All patient details in this demonstration are fictional.

Enter what happened. Review the timeline. Correct anything missing. Download a visit summary and bring the original test report.

The benefit is having important details together when it is time to explain them.

For someone uncomfortable speaking English, that conversation can feel overwhelming. People can write in their preferred language or mix languages.

With permission, optional external AI helps organize an English summary alongside the original for review. It does not diagnose illness or replace a qualified interpreter. The English organizer also works without external AI.

Now look at what the bill misses.

Our fictional patient paid 180 dollars for care and 45 dollars for travel, missed three workdays, and received eight hours of family help.

The record shows 225 dollars paid. Workdays and caregiving hours stay separate.

Download the appointment and cost summary. Use the care-access checklist for questions to a clinic or billing office.

A total alone will not pay a bill. A clear record can help someone explain what support they need.

What does this mean for a community?

Our intended organizational users are county health departments and community partners. They need to decide where to focus limited resources.

Community Burden and Action brings together New York Lyme surveillance, Census population and language data, HRSA healthcare resources, CDC tick surveillance, and CDC PLACES community-health estimates. CMS adds separate Original Medicare payment context—not Lyme-specific spending.

Here is the calculation: 1,060 Dutchess cases reported in 2024, multiplied by Hook’s societal-cost benchmark of 2,032 dollars, gives about 2.15 million dollars in 2016 dollars.

We show the source and formula. It is an illustration—not measured county spending or proven savings.

Then we move from information to a possible action.

Health departments already share prevention information at local events. A partnership could help residents leave with an outdoor plan, a visit note, or questions about affordable care.

A future secure pilot, with explicit consent, could document unmet needs. Resource information would remain available without contributing personal information.

Partners could consider those findings alongside public data, confirm available resources, and track referrals completed, appointments reached, and needs still unmet. Personal notes are not automatically sent to counties.

People living with Lyme disease and a county health commissioner reviewed the working app. Their feedback helped us simplify instructions and make support resources available without entering a story.

We have invited Mandarin-, Spanish-, and English-speaking reviewers to test clarity and meaning. That work is ongoing.

The pilot app is publicly accessible. We plan to reach users through patient groups, health centers, and local public-health partners. Our proposed model keeps patient access free through grants and partnerships.

Next, we want a small partner pilot to test usefulness and support connections before claiming better outcomes or savings. The same record structure could be adapted to other illnesses, with condition-specific review. Scan the code to try the app or discuss a pilot.
