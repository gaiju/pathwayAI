As a pathologist, I studied disease through a microscope. As an epidemiologist, I studied patterns across communities. Then it became personal.

After I lost my job, my family lost our health insurance. The next month, my husband was bitten by a tick and became ill with Lyme disease. Where could we find affordable care? What would the tests cost? How could he explain his symptoms in a language he did not feel comfortable speaking? I also helped another family navigate disability benefits. I saw how overwhelming paperwork becomes when someone is ill and English is limited.

Those lessons led me to build PathwayAI. It connects prevention, preparation, language assistance, local resources, and household burden—with particular attention to immigrants, underserved minority communities, and people facing financial barriers.

Let me show you. Choose the situation that fits you. “Quick and easy” keeps steps short. “More detail” provides additional explanations. Choose only the steps you need.

First, prevention. People planning time outdoors can find prevention information and download a preparation plan. If someone finds a tick, the tick-bite page provides removal instructions and guidance on when to contact a clinician. Timely, appropriate treatment can help prevent Lyme disease complications. But knowing you need care does not mean you know how to get it—especially without insurance.

Delayed care can lead to complications and greater costs. Helping people overcome barriers to timely care is also a community priority.

In Care & Support, people can find community health centers and prepare questions about income-based fees, coverage, and financial assistance. They confirm services, costs, and language support with the provider. Next, organize what happened.

People can write in their preferred language—even mixed languages, keeping English place names or medical terms. If they choose, built-in AI assistance helps organize an English summary alongside their original words for review. The app asks permission before sending text to an AI service.

It helps people prepare for conversations with their clinicians and qualified interpreters.

The summary brings together exposure, symptoms, tests, and care received. Symptom onset and diagnosis delay stay separate.

AI can organize a story, but it cannot replace human judgment. Public data can reveal patterns, but it cannot tell a family’s whole story.

Patients remain at the center.

How does this help communities?

PathwayAI combines CDC, Census, HRSA, and CMS open data with New York Lyme surveillance to describe disease patterns, barriers, and healthcare resources.

Dutchess County reported 1,060 Lyme cases in 2024. Applying the Hook study’s average societal cost gives an illustrative burden of approximately 2.15 million dollars, in 2016 dollars—not measured county spending.

Counties could introduce PathwayAI through websites and local events.

Our next step is secure, consent-based reporting with a community partner. We’re looking for one high-incidence county health department and one community organization to co-design a six-month pilot.

Feedback from people affected by Lyme disease and a county health commissioner has shaped the app. We also thank our English-, Mandarin-, and Spanish-speaking testers for helping make PathwayAI easier to use across language backgrounds.

We seek partners to evaluate usefulness and support connections, with grants and partnerships proposed to keep patient access free.
